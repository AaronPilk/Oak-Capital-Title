For buyers & sellers

What title insurance actually does for you.

It shows up as a line item on your closing statement and most people sign it without knowing what they bought. Here is the plain version — what it covers, what it does not, who pays, and what it costs.

If you are buying

Two questions have to be answered before the keys change hands: does the seller genuinely own what they are selling, and is there anything attached to the property that follows it to you?

Why it matters. A defect in the title can restrict how you use the land, cost you real money to defend, or in a bad case cost you the property. Our search goes through the public record to confirm the ownership is clean before you sign, and the policy stands behind what the record could not show.

You pay the premium once. There is no renewal, no expiry, and no annual bill. The protection lasts as long as you — or whoever inherits from you — hold an interest in the property.

If you are selling

Your job is to deliver clean title on the day. Ours is to find anything standing in the way early enough to fix it quietly — an old second mortgage that was paid but never released, a contractor's lien, a name spelled wrong on a deed in 1994.

The plain version
The basics

Insurance that looks backwards, not forwards

Every other policy you own covers something that might happen next — a fire, a crash, a hospital stay. Title insurance is the opposite. It covers things that already happened, before you ever saw the house, and that nobody caught in time.

When you take out a mortgage, your lender requires a policy protecting their stake. You can buy a second one protecting yours. That one is optional, and it is the one worth understanding.

“Title” is just the legal word for who owns a piece of property. A title insurance policy covers claims on that ownership from someone other than you — the classic example being a construction company that never got paid for work done under a previous owner, and still has a lien on the place to prove it.

The claim can land at any point. You might own the house peacefully for eleven years before it surfaces. That is because the person with the competing right often does not know they have it either — an overlooked heir, for instance, may have no idea a share of your property is legally theirs until someone goes looking through an old will.

Before your loan closes, the lender orders a title search. We go through the public record looking for anything that could undercut your rights or theirs. Three things come up most often:

Liens

A contractor, a tax office or a lender who was never paid can attach a claim to the property. It stays with the land, not the person — which is how buyers end up holding a previous owner's unpaid bill.

Easements

Someone else's legal right to use land you own. Usually harmless — a utility corridor along the back fence. Occasionally the reason you cannot build where you planned to build.

Encumbrances

The wider category: liens and easements, plus zoning rules, HOA covenants and a tenant's right to stay put. Anything that limits what you can do with the property you just bought.

The search reaches further than most people expect: deeds and mortgages, but also divorce decrees, court judgments, tax records and child support orders. Ownership leaves a trail through all of them.

When something turns up — the industry calls these “clouds” on title — we work to clear it. Some clouds are a phone call and a corrected document. Others need the seller to act, which usually means the two agents sorting it out between them. And a small number are serious enough to end a deal, which is far better than discovering them a year after you move in.

Coverage

Where the policy helps — and where it stops

The line is simpler than it looks. Problems that existed before you bought are covered. Problems you create afterwards are not.

Covered

  • Errors in the property survey
  • Boundary disputes with a neighbour
  • Mistakes on the deed itself
  • Building code violations left by a previous owner
  • Conflicting wills and overlooked heirs
  • An ex-spouse who never signed off on the sale
  • Forged signatures and forged documents
  • Liens from contractors, tax offices or old lenders
  • Encroachments across the property line
  • Documents recorded incorrectly

Not covered

A title policy is not a general shield around your property rights. In particular it will not cover title problems you cause yourself:

  • Not paying the company that replaced your roof
  • Falling behind on your property taxes
  • Eminent domain — a government taking land for public use

The test to remember: would this have changed your mind about buying, had you known at the time? If yes, that is the territory a title policy is built for. If it started after closing, it is not.

Standard or expanded?

Expanded coverage widens the policy to take in additional risks beyond the standard form. Whether it earns its cost depends entirely on the property in front of you. Call us with the address and we will tell you straight which one we would buy in your position.

Cost & who pays
The money

What you actually pay

An owner's policy does three things when it is needed. It pays off a lien nobody found. It funds the legal defense when somebody claims a right to your property. And where a deed turns out to be forged, it settles in cash with the buyer who bought in good faith. Less dramatically, it protects your ability to sell one day — because the next buyer's search will find whatever yours missed.

The lender's policy does none of that for you, which makes it a strange thing to be asked to pay for. Here is the logic anyway: if the property was sold to you fraudulently and you lose it, the lender files a claim to recover the payments they were counting on. Normally they would foreclose to cover a loss — but if the house was never the seller's to sell, foreclosure is not available to them.

How much

One payment at closing. Nothing recurring. The owner's policy is priced off the purchase price; the lender's off the loan amount. Together they typically run 0.5% to 1.0% of the purchase price — roughly $1,500 to $3,000 on a $300,000 home, according to ALTA.

Worth knowing in the Carolinas: the premium is the same whichever agency you use. Price is not the variable. Who runs your search, how deep they go and whether they answer the phone in October — those are the variables. Run your own numbers on the rate calculator.

Who pays

The buyer covers the lender's policy as part of closing costs. The owner's policy can be paid by either side on the buyer's behalf — in practice, local custom in your market usually settles it before anyone negotiates.

One thing to ask for: buying both policies at the same time qualifies for a “simultaneous issue” charge, which brings the cost of the owner's policy down. It is not automatic in every conversation, so raise it.
